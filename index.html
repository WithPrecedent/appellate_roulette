    What is appellate roulette?¶

    The rule of law rests on a promise of generality: like cases will be treated alike, and the outcome of a legal dispute will turn on what the law requires rather than on the identity of the official who happens to apply it. This study subjects that promise to empirical scrutiny in federal criminal appeals.

    • Cases: the opinions of the twelve regional courts of appeals filed since January 1, 2010, from CourtListener, parsed and coded with courtpy.
    • Judges: who each judge of a panel is, who appointed the judge, and the judge's sex, race, age, and career, from the Federal Judicial Center's Biographical Directory of Article III Federal Judges.
    • Analysis: with amos, which records every step and parameter, so that the results can be reproduced.

    Research design¶

    The study's methods are described in one settings file, studies/appellate_roulette.toml:
    Unit One criminal appeal brought by the defendant and decided by a three-judge panel, all of whose judges are identified.
    Outcome outcome_criminal_defendant_won: whether the court reversed, vacated, or remanded.
    Treatment Which judges heard the appeal (panel_names), and the composition of the panel: how many of its judges were appointed by Democratic presidents, are women, are not white, were prosecutors or public defenders, are senior, or are visiting from another court, and their mean age.
    Comparison Appeals of the same circuit and year, among which the judges are drawn without regard to the merits.

    It asks whether the outcome depends on the panel in three ways:

    1. The wheel (roulette, in roulette.py). How far the outcomes of each judge's appeals, and of each kind of panel, are from the outcomes of the other appeals of the same circuit and year is measured.
    2. The odds (fixest). A model of whether the defendant won, with a fixed effect for each circuit and each year, estimates how much one more judge of each kind on the panel changes the odds.
    3. Prediction (the analyst). Multiple models are trained to predict the outcome from the panel and the appeal, and tested on appeals that were held out. If the panel does not matter, shuffling its composition costs the best model nothing (permutation_importance).

    The opinions¶

    CourtListener publishes its database as bulk files every three months. courtpy's own bulk download reads five of them, because CourtListener records the court of a case on its docket. This project's load_opinions (and the appellate-roulette opinions command) reads only the two that hold opinions:

    File Size (2026-09-30) Read
    opinions 55 GB Yes: the text of each opinion.
    opinion-clusters 2.5 GB Yes: each decision's name, date, judges, and status.
    dockets 5.1 GB No.
    citations 0.1 GB No.
    courts under 1 MB No.

    The file of opinions is nearly all of the download, and there is no smaller bulk file that holds the opinions of only some courts. What reading two files saves is mostly time: they are decompressed and read with every core of the computer (with indexed_bzip2 and pyarrow), which takes from half an hour to an hour rather than many hours. To download less, use the API (below).

    Without the dockets, the court of each opinion is found from the opinion itself, by the rules in instructions/courts.csv: the website that CourtListener collected the opinion from (such as ca1.uscourts.gov), or else the court that the headmatter of its case names. Measured against the dockets, for the 375,994 decisions of the thirteen courts of appeals filed from 2010 through September 2026, the rules:
    Find the court of 346,441 decisions (92.1%), and every decision filed since 2020
    Put in the wrong court of appeals none
    Take for a court of appeals 4 opinions of other courts
    Find by year 83% of 2010, 85 to 89% of 2011 to 2017, 91% of 2018, 97.5% of 2019
    Find by court 64% of the D.C. Circuit, 85% of the Fourth, 86% of the Seventh, 88% of the Federal, and 92 to 98% of the others

    The decisions that are not found are the 29,423 that CourtListener has only from the Harvard Law School Library's scans of the printed reporters, which come with neither an address nor headmatter. About three fifths of them have the same name as (or a shorter form of the name of) a decision of the same court and day that is found, so they appear to be second copies. About a quarter (some 7,600, or 2% of all decisions) have no counterpart. Those are concentrated in 2010 and 2018, and a third of them are filed under the D.C. Circuit, where many are opinions of the district court that the dockets attribute to the court of appeals.

    Two ways to add the decisions that are left out, both of which skip the cases that are already saved:

    # With a CourtListener API key, which is stored in the system's keyring.
# About ten hours of requests at the API's pace, which can be stopped and
# resumed.
uv run appellate-roulette key set
uv run appellate-roulette download api --courts federal_circuits --start 2010-01-01 --folder data/court_listener

# Or from the bulk data with its dockets (5 GB more, and slower to read).
uv run appellate-roulette download bulk --courts federal_circuits --start 2010-01-01 --folder data/court_listener

    Delete data/appeals.csv afterward, so that the added cases are parsed. To collect every case from the API instead, with no bulk files at all, set download = "api" in the settings (and remove parties, which only the bulk extraction uses).

    CourtListener also holds some decisions more than once: an opinion that a court posted twice, or an opinion and an order in one appeal. About 3% of the decisions that name the United States repeat the court, day, and name of another. An appeal that is counted twice has the same judges and the same outcome both times, which would make the judges look as if they mattered, so the study keeps one decision for each court, day, and name (drop_duplicates).

    The variables¶

    Variable Meaning From
    outcome_criminal_defendant_won Whether the defendant won: the court reversed, vacated, or remanded in the defendant's appeal. courtpy's code_outcome
    type_criminal, appeal_by_defendant Whether the case is criminal, and whether the defendant brought the appeal. courtpy's code_case_type and code_parties
    panel_names, panel_found, panel_size The judges of the panel as the roster names them, how many were found, and how many the opinion names. courtpy's code_judges
    democratic_appointees, women, minority_judges, former_prosecutors, former_defenders, visiting_judges, senior_judges How many of the three judges are of each kind. The settings (derive_columns), from the roster's attributes
    panel_age The mean age of the judges. courtpy's code_judges
    criminal_*, civil_habeas, civil_aedpa The crimes and proceedings that the opinion discusses. courtpy's federal rules
    issue_* The issues of the appeal, such as suppression, sufficiency of the evidence, or the reasonableness of a sentence. studies/criminal_issues.csv

    Getting started¶

    Requirements¶

    The study requires Python 3.11, 3.12, or 3.13 and uv. It runs on Linux, macOS, and Windows.

    The first run downloads the two bulk files of opinions (about 58 GB, kept outside the project so that they can be used again) unless courtpy has already downloaded them, and the Federal Judicial Center's files of judges. No API key is needed.

    Installation¶

    In the project's folder:

    uv sync

    courtpy is installed from GitHub until version 0.2.2 is on PyPI (see [tool.uv.sources] in pyproject.toml).

    Usage¶

    Make the roster of judges once, and then run the study:

    uv run appellate-roulette judges
uv run appellate-roulette run studies/appellate_roulette.toml --export

    In Python:

    import appellate_roulette

project = appellate_roulette.Project.create("studies/appellate_roulette.toml")
print(project.result.tables["balance"])
print(project.result.tables["roulette"])
print(project.result.tables["roulette_judges"].head(20))

    appellate-roulette also runs any courtpy command (such as appellate-roulette rules studies/criminal_issues.csv) with this project's techniques available. To extract the opinions without running the study:

    uv run appellate-roulette opinions --courts federal_circuits --parties "United States"

    The first run saves:

    File Holds
    data/court_listener/ One JSON file for each case, with its opinions.
    data/appeals.csv The coded cases. Later runs reuse it; delete it after changing the load_opinions_parameters section or the rules.
    results/ The tables, figures, and report of the analysis, with --export.

    The tables to read first:

    Table Holds
    balance For each thing that was true of an appeal before it was heard, the p-values of whether the judges and the composition of the panels predict it.
    roulette The test of the judges, the joint test of the composition, and a test of each attribute: the statistic, what chance alone would make it, and the p-value.
    roulette_judges For each judge, how many appeals the judge heard, how often the defendant won, how often the defendant won in the other appeals of the same circuits and years, and how unusual the difference is.
    fixest_coefficients The change in the log odds for one more judge of each kind.
    permutation_importance How much the best model's predictions worsen when each feature is shuffled.

    Layout¶

    Path Holds
    studies/ The settings of the study, and the rules for the issues of criminal appeals.
    src/appellate_roulette/opinions.py load_opinions and OpinionData, which extract the opinions.
    src/appellate_roulette/instructions/courts.csv The rules that find the court of an opinion.
    src/appellate_roulette/roulette.py roulette, balance, and spin, which test whether the draw of judges matters.
    tests/ Tests, with fictional judges and cases.

    Acknowledgments¶

    The opinions come from CourtListener, a project of the Free Law Project, whose bulk data makes research on court opinions possible at no cost, and some of them from the Harvard Law School Library's Caselaw Access Project. The judges' biographies come from the Federal Judicial Center.
